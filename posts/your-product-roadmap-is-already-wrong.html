Your Product Roadmap Is Already Wrong

April 7, 2026 · 8:00 AM CT

Most product roadmaps are now wrong.

AI has changed all of the timelines, budgets and features... most executives just don't know it yet.

Every executive I talk to still uses the phrase "when AI matures." They say it like it's weather on the horizon — something to prepare for, budget against, and train the team on over the next fiscal year.

The storm already hit. The flood waters are coming. If you haven't built your boat... climb a tree.

The way you reason about features, timing, and cost has already changed. Most of the product plans sitting on executive desks right now were built with 2023 math. That math is not wrong in some future tense. It is wrong today.

A feature that used to justify a six-person team for a quarter is now a weekend for one engineer with the right model. A category that lived behind a moat of proprietary data is a wrapper away from being commoditized. A service priced at $200 an hour of human judgment now competes with something that answers in three seconds for a tenth of a cent. These are not predictions. This is what the tools on my desk did this week.

The people building the models are describing the same shift.

Jensen Huang at GTC: the agentic era is already in production. Roughly a trillion dollars of NVIDIA chip orders are priced against that reality through 2027.

Dario Amodei at Davos: half of entry-level white-collar jobs could be gone inside five years. Anthropic's run rate went from $9B to $20B in the three months he was saying it.

Sam Altman, on repeat to enterprise buyers: bolting AI onto your existing operating model is not the play. Rebuild around it, or be rebuilt by someone who did.

None of them are warning about something coming. They are describing something that has already landed, and watching the market price it in a year late.

The executives who are going to survive this are the ones who stop asking "what happens when AI gets good enough" and start asking "what in our current plan is already obsolete." That is not a strategy session. It is a teardown.

And the only people qualified to run that teardown are the ones who have been in the tools this week. Not last quarter. Not last fiscal year. The models changed twice while you were reading this.

The transition is not in front of you. It is behind you.

It is a race. Time to catch up.
