AI's DSP Moment

June 25, 2026 · 8:00 AM CT

In 1983, Texas Instruments shipped a chip that did almost nothing. It could not run a program or a spreadsheet. It did one job — the signal math behind audio and video sampling and compression — because running that job on a general-purpose CPU was too slow and too expensive. The DSP chip was born, and it ran the next thirty years of media. Thank DSP chips for every Zoom call you have ever enjoyed and every cell phone call you have ever made.

We are running that playbook again, and most AI strategies have not noticed.

Start with the silicon. Elon Musk's Terafab, announced in March as a Tesla, SpaceX and xAI venture, is not trying to out-NVIDIA NVIDIA on general-purpose GPUs. It is building purpose-specific chips for cars, robots and space, where Tesla claims its AI5 part matches an H100 at a fraction of the cost and power. Google, Amazon and a wave of startups are doing the same with inference-only silicon. The general-purpose chip was never the cheap way to run one job. It was the only way — until someone built the specialist.

Now watch the same move climb up the stack to the models. Why pay for a frontier model that can write a symphony and generate video just to summarize your email? NVIDIA's own researchers published the answer last year: small, specialized models are the future of agentic AI — ten to thirty times cheaper to run, and good enough for most of what an agent actually does.

Here is the part to internalize. The edge stops being "which one big model do we buy." It becomes "how well do we route every task to the cheapest thing that can do it." Granular specialization is coming. Profitability will belong to whoever can run a fleet of small models and agents — not whoever rents the biggest one.

History does not repeat. It re-fabs.

It is a race. Time to catch up.
